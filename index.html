Scientists Confirm Mediterranean Diet Adds Years to Life

A landmark 30-year study finds plant-rich diets reduce cardiovascular disease risk by 40%.

A sweeping 30-year study tracking 120,000 adults across 15 countries has confirmed what nutritionists have long suspected: following a Mediterranean-style diet rich in olive oil, legumes, whole grains, and fresh vegetables can meaningfully extend healthy lifespan.

Participants who adhered closely to the diet saw a 40% reduction in cardiovascular events and a 22% lower risk of cognitive decline compared to those eating processed-food-heavy diets. Researchers from the University of Athens called it "the most conclusive dietary evidence ever assembled."

The findings, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, are already influencing national nutrition guidelines across Europe and North America, with several governments pledging subsidies for fresh produce to make healthy eating more accessible to low-income families.

Humpback Whale Population Rebounds to Pre-Whaling Numbers

Conservation efforts pay off as global humpback whale count surpasses 80,000 for the first time in a century.

Marine biologists are celebrating a monumental conservation victory: the global humpback whale population has rebounded past 80,000 individuals, effectively recovering to pre-commercial-whaling levels for the first time since the 19th century.

The recovery is credited to a combination of international whaling bans, expanded marine protected areas, and reduced ocean noise pollution from shipping route adjustments. The South Atlantic population alone has grown at 11% per year for the past decade.

"This is proof that when humanity commits to protecting a species, nature responds," said Dr. Sylvia Mendez of the Marine Conservation Institute. The milestone is being hailed as a blueprint for the recovery of other endangered whale species, including the North Atlantic right whale.

Teen Inventor's Solar Desalination Device Wins Global Prize

A 17-year-old from Kenya has built a low-cost solar device that purifies 50 litres of water per day for remote villages.

Amara Odhiambo, a 17-year-old from Kisumu, Kenya, has won the Global Youth Innovation Prize for designing a solar-powered desalination unit built entirely from recycled materials that costs less than $30 to construct.

The device, which she calls 'Jua Safi' (Clean Sun in Swahili), uses concentrated sunlight to evaporate and re-condense saline or contaminated groundwater, producing up to 50 litres of clean drinking water per day — enough for a small family. She built the first prototype in her school's workshop over three months.

The prize committee awarded her the $100,000 top prize, which Amara says she will invest in manufacturing 500 units for communities across the East African Rift Valley. Three NGOs have already pledged matching funds.

World's Largest Coral Reef Restoration Project Declares Success

The Great Barrier Reef's northern section has regained 65% of its historical coral cover after a decade of restoration.

Australian marine scientists announced this week that the northern section of the Great Barrier Reef has achieved 65% of its historical coral cover, marking the most successful large-scale reef restoration effort ever undertaken.

The decade-long project used a combination of coral gardening, heat-resistant coral breeding, and reef-safe sunscreen regulations alongside strict fishing protections. Over 14 million coral fragments were grown in underwater nurseries and transplanted by a coalition of reef scientists, indigenous rangers, and trained volunteers.

The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority called it "a turning point in the history of coral conservation." Scientists from 22 countries attended the announcement ceremony held on the reef itself, arriving by research vessel rather than aircraft to minimize their carbon footprint.

Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority

New Gene Therapy Restores Hearing in Deaf Children

A single-dose gene therapy has enabled 94% of children with hereditary deafness to hear conversational speech for the first time.

A phase-3 clinical trial published in Nature Medicine has reported stunning results: a single-dose gene therapy targeting the OTOF gene mutation — the most common cause of hereditary deafness — restored functional hearing in 94% of treated children within six months of treatment.

The therapy, developed jointly by researchers at Johns Hopkins and the Karolinska Institute, delivers a corrected copy of the gene directly into the cochlea's hair cells using a modified adeno-associated virus. Treated children progressed from profound deafness to being able to hear normal conversational speech and most environmental sounds.

Regulatory filings for emergency approval have been submitted in the US and EU. If approved, the one-time treatment could eventually replace cochlear implants for children with this specific genetic variant, affecting an estimated 200,000 people worldwide.

City of Amsterdam Goes Completely Car-Free in Historic Centre

Amsterdam's medieval core is now a pedestrian and cyclist paradise after its last combustion-engine car was removed.

Amsterdam has achieved a milestone that urban planners worldwide have been watching: the city's entire historic centre is now fully free of private combustion-engine vehicles, following a gradual decade-long transition that concluded this spring.

The transformation replaced car lanes with widened cycle paths, urban gardens, and a network of quiet electric trams. Delivery logistics are handled via hydrogen-powered cargo boats on the canal network and electric cargo bikes. Air quality in the area has improved by 60% since the programme began, and pedestrian footfall in local shops has increased by 35%.

City mayor Rosa van der Berg said the project had "proved that the liveable city is not a dream." More than 40 cities across Europe and Asia have sent delegations to study the model, with Seoul and Lisbon announcing similar programmes.

Random Act of Kindness Chain Spans 47 Countries

A coffee shop tab left open by one customer in Dublin has triggered a global chain of 1.2 million pay-it-forward gestures.

What began with a Dublin barista posting a note about a customer who pre-paid for 200 strangers' coffees has snowballed into an international pay-it-forward movement spanning 47 countries and documented 1.2 million individual acts of kindness in just six weeks.

The original customer, who asked to remain anonymous, said she simply wanted to brighten some mornings. The story went viral when regulars at the Portobello café started leaving notes sharing how a free coffee had changed their difficult days. Inspired people worldwide began their own chains — from paying for groceries behind them in supermarkets to settling strangers' library fines.

A social scientist at University College Dublin tracking the phenomenon says it illustrates "remarkable human generosity latency" — the idea that many people want to be kind but simply need a first mover to unlock collective generosity.

Paralympic Sprinter Breaks 100m World Record in World Athletics Final

Kesi Mwangi clocked 9.94 seconds in the T44 100m, becoming the first para-athlete to run sub-10 seconds.

In one of the most electric moments in athletics history, Kenyan sprinter Kesi Mwangi ran the 100 metres in 9.94 seconds at the World Athletics Para Championships in Doha on Wednesday, becoming the first Paralympic athlete to run under 10 seconds in the T44 category (single below-knee amputation).

The packed Khalifa Stadium erupted as the time flashed on the scoreboard — a time that would have qualified for Olympic finals as recently as the 1990s. Mwangi, who lost his lower left leg to cancer at age 12, has been on a record-breaking streak this season, shaving 0.08 seconds off his previous best.

"I want children who look like me to see what's possible," Mwangi said through tears in the post-race interview. The performance generated more global social media engagement than any athletics event in the past five years.

AI Tool Deciphers Ancient Herculaneum Scrolls Burned by Vesuvius

Researchers used machine learning to read 2,000-year-old philosophical texts carbonised in the 79 AD eruption.

An international team of classicists and computer scientists has announced the successful decipherment of 15 previously unreadable Herculaneum papyrus scrolls, using an AI model trained on X-ray micro-CT scans to detect subtle ink-thickness variations inside the carbonised rolls.

The scrolls, buried when Mount Vesuvius erupted in 79 AD, contain extensive passages from an unknown Epicurean philosophical treatise discussing the nature of friendship and happiness — concepts strikingly relevant to contemporary wellbeing research, scholars say. Two complete works thought to be lost forever have now been identified in the collection.

The project, a collaboration between Oxford, Naples, and the Kentucky-based Vesuvius Challenge team, has made all deciphered texts freely available online. Classics scholars worldwide called it "the greatest literary recovery since the Renaissance."

Town Discovers 94-Year-Old Never Had a Birthday Party — Throws Epic One

Entire village of 3,000 turns out to celebrate Edith Hartley's first-ever birthday party with bunting, a brass band, and cake.

When volunteers at a community befriending charity discovered that 94-year-old Edith Hartley of Ludlow, Shropshire, had never in her life had a birthday party, they decided to fix that immediately.

Within three weeks, the entire town mobilised. Local schoolchildren painted 94 banners. The Ludlow Brass Band rehearsed her favourite hymns. Bakeries competed to donate the best cake. On the day of her birthday, over 3,000 people lined the high street as Edith was driven through in a flower-covered vintage car, wearing a crown the local jeweller had made specially.

"I thought they were all there for something else," Edith reportedly said when she saw the crowd. "Then I saw my name on the banners." The story has been shared millions of times online, with birthday cards arriving from 54 countries. Edith says it was "better than anything I could have imagined."

Shropshire Star
